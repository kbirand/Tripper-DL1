Tripper Hardware · e-bike edition

Tripper Puck

A matchbox-sized GPS + IMU + barometer telemetry puck that streams to the Tripper app over BLE and reads the bike's own CAN bus. Powered from the bike through a 5 V BEC — no battery, no SD card. Headless by design: the phone is its recorder, the OLED is a convenience.

Budget ≈ ₺3.700 core Size rear ≈ 55 × 40 × 22 mm + bar box Power bike 5 V BEC Build time 1 weekend

The concept

Why this stays small: the entire physical interface is four things.

Everything else — live dashboard, reviewing rides, naming, exporting — already exists in the Tripper app.

Scope pivot — e-bike edition (2026-07-21, CAN added 2026-07-23) The puck targets the e-bike: powered from the bike through a 5 V BEC (no LiPo, no slide switch) and BLE-first with no microSD — the phone records through the Tripper app's recorder pipeline, so the old v2 BLE link is the product. Consequences: LiPo + SD module + slide switch + NeoPixel are gone, the auto-record state machine moves into the app, and the puck becomes a stateless sensor that boots with the bike. It gained a second input path: a listen-only SN65HVD230 CAN transceiver on D8/D9 reads the bike's own bus (battery %, pack voltage, cell balance, motor current, kickstand, ride mode, regen level) straight into the telemetry packet. Status lives entirely on the 0.91" OLED; the two buttons are D1 (screens) and D2 (marker / mount-zero). Trade-off accepted with open eyes: no BLE link = no data for that stretch — the SD "black box" is gone; re-add it later if a lost ride ever hurts. Verify the BEC holds 5 V at standstill and doesn't drop out at low load.
Design rule Resist adding screens, menus, or more buttons. Every feature that needs a UI belongs in the phone app, where it's already built and glove-tested.

Start with the Light build It is the entire telemetry product — fused orientation, g-force, barometric altitude and every decoded bike-CAN signal — in four parts, and your phone already carries a GPS the app already reads. The Full build buys 5 Hz position (the phone's is 1 Hz), a dashboard readable with the phone pocketed, and two hardware buttons.

The Full build is strictly additive. The handlebar parts land on pins the Light build leaves free (D1, D2, D6, D7), so getting there is an upgrade rather than a rebuild — nothing already soldered gets moved. The BLE packets are byte-identical across both, so the app needs no branch: a Light puck reports its GPS block as invalid, the same state a Full puck reports before it gets a fix, and a capability byte in the status packet says which is on the other end.

Two I²C buses, because the display moved to the handlebar The sensors ride at the rear and the OLED sits on the bar, about a metre of cable apart. Sharing one bus would drag the BNO055 — already the fragile part, already forcing the whole bus to 100 kHz — onto that long, capacitive, electrically noisy run. So they're split: bus 0 (Wire, D4/D5) stays 15 cm long inside the rear box with the BNO055 and BMP280; bus 1 (Wire1, D3/D10) carries only the OLED out to the bar.

The asymmetry is the point. A metre of cable can glitch a screen and nobody loses data; the same metre on the sensor bus costs you the ride. Firmware: Wire1.begin(D3, D10), idling at OLED_BUS_HZ (100 kHz) and bursting frames at OLED_BURST_HZ (400 kHz). If the display tears or drops out, drop the burst to 100 kHz first — that one constant is the whole debug story.
Rules for the handlebar run — a metre of I²C is not free

The handlebar box is 3.3 V only. Both things in it — the SSD1306 and the NEO-8M carrier — run off the XIAO's own 3V3 regulator. The only 5 V in the whole build is the BEC feeding VUSB inside the rear box; never run it up to the bar. That also sharpens the rules below: there's no regulator at the far end to absorb cable drop, so whatever the metre of wire loses comes straight out of the GPS's supply margin.

  • Shielded multicore, shield grounded at the rear end only. Both ends makes a loop.
  • SDA and SCL each twisted with their own GND return — never twisted together, they crosstalk. Same for GPS TX/RX.
  • 2.2 kΩ pull-ups on the OLED bus, fitted at the ESP32 end, replacing the module's 4.7–10 kΩ. A metre adds ~100 pF and weak pull-ups can't drag the edges up through it.
  • 10 kΩ to 3V3 + 100 nF to GND on D1 and D2, at the ESP32 end. The internal pull-up is ~45 kΩ; a metre of wire on that is an antenna for BEC and controller switching noise, and you get phantom presses.
  • 100 µF at the handlebar end, across 3V3/GND near the GPS. Its acquisition current spikes into the cable inductance otherwise sag the rail and lengthen time-to-fix.
  • ≥ 26 AWG on 3V3 and GND. The bar end draws ~60 mA — only ~16 mV of drop at 26 AWG, but thin 30 AWG hookup wire more than doubles it, off a rail with no headroom to spare.
  • Longer than a metre? The pull-up and the bus capacitance set the rise time, which must stay under 1 µs at 100 kHz, while the sink current (3.3 V ÷ R) must stay under 3 mA. At ~100 pF per metre that gives 2.2 kΩ to ~2 m and 1.5 kΩ (2.2 mA sink) to ~3 m; past 3 m the 400 pF bus-capacitance ceiling binds and no pull-up value saves it. Beyond 2 m also pin OLED_BURST_HZ at 100 kHz.
  • Don't reach for an I²C buffer. The arithmetic above covers any run a motorcycle actually needs, and both usual parts are unobtainable domestically — the P82B715 is obsolete and the P82B96 shows zero stock at 4–6 weeks' lead. Switching the display to SPI to dodge the issue is worse: it turns an 8-core cable into a 12-core one, and 12 × 0.14 mm² shielded is ~7.5 mm of stiff cable that will fatigue at the steering head.
  • Keep the cable thin. 8 × 0.14 mm² (≈26 AWG) shielded is ~6 mm OD and stays flexible. 0.14 mm² carries the ~60 mA with room to spare, so there is no reason to go thicker and every reason not to.
GPS UART needs none of this — 115200 baud push-pull point-to-point is happy over several metres. And moving the GPS to the bar satisfies the ≥ 15 mm antenna rule for free, since the ESP32 stays at the rear.
The BNO055 + ESP32 I²C clock-stretch problem — read before you solder The BNO055 stretches the I²C clock in a way ESP32 I²C peripherals handle badly. It's the single most-reported gotcha with this chip. The usual escape is to run the BNO055 over UART instead. The Gravity connector is VCC/GND/SDA/SCL, I²C only — but correction (2026-07-21, verified on the physical V1.0 board): the back-side pad row does break out PS1/PS2 (plus NBOOT/RST/INT/I2C_ADDR/BL_IND — no SDA/SCL pads), so a UART fallback technically exists via the PS pads. It would strand the I²C-only BMP280 on a separate bus, and the clean 10-minute hammer test made it moot — noted here in case a future revision ever needs it.

Mitigations, in order: drop the bus to 100 kHz (Wire.setClock(100000)), raise the I²C timeout (Wire.setTimeOut()), keep SDA/SCL runs short with 4.7 kΩ pull-ups. This is workable — but prove it on the breadboard before the case is glued (build step 2).
If every I²C device looks dead at once — check the pad, not the wiring The first XIAO used in this project had its D4 pad clamped low: it read 0 even with the internal pull-up enabled and nothing attached, shorted to ground somewhere on the board. An I²C line that cannot idle high is a bus no device can ever signal on, so the symptom is four simultaneously dead sensors — which reads like a wiring disaster or four bad modules, not one bad pin. SDA ran on D3 as a workaround until that board was replaced; the pin map above is back on the default pads.

How to tell a dead pad from a wiring fault: pull the pin up, then down, and read it back. A pad the chip still controls follows both (1 then 0). One that reads 0 against the internal pull-up is being held to ground by something outside the GPIO. Sweep the whole pad row rather than testing one pin — a single pin reading oddly is a story about a broken test, but the same test passing on six neighbours and failing on one names the fault. padSweep() in firmware/bench_imu_can/ does exactly this on every boot, and firmware/i2c_diag/ localises it further with normal and swapped bus scans.
Run it 6-axis (IMU mode), not 9-axis (NDOF) NDOF mode needs a calibrated magnetometer. On a motorcycle — steel frame, alternator, speaker magnets — that calibration is junk and yaw will wander. You don't need it: Tripper takes heading from GPS, and the reference-quaternion math makes absolute yaw irrelevant. OPERATION_MODE_IMUPLUS sidesteps the BNO055's worst weakness. The magnetometer is the one part of this board you paid for and won't use.
Antenna rule (this decides your GPS quality) Mount the GPS patch antenna at the top of the handlebar enclosure, facing the sky. The ≥ 15 mm separation from the ESP32 comes free now that the module lives at the rear — but keep it clear of the OLED ribbon and the USB cable too.

Connectors — two harnesses, deliberately different

Two enclosures means two cable runs. They're different sizes on purpose: you cannot cross-plug them.

RunConductorsConnector
Bike → rear box+5 V, GND, CAN_H, CAN_LM8 4-pin, IP67
Rear box → handlebar box3V3, GND, SDA, SCL, GPS TX, GPS RX, btn1, btn2M12 8-pin A-coded, IP67

The two runs carry different voltages — 5 V into the rear box, 3.3 V onward to the bar — which is a second reason to keep the connectors different sizes.

Gender follows the live side Whichever end stays energised when the pair comes apart gets the female sockets — recessed contacts can't short against the frame. On the bike run that's the cable (it runs back to the BEC), so the rear enclosure carries a male panel receptacle and the cable carries a female plug. The handlebar run is dead on both sides once the bike connector is pulled, so either orientation is safe there; keep the panel male for consistency.

Parts verified in stock domestically (July 2026): rear panel ATTEND 219A-04MSR ≈ ₺243, bike cable SIGNAL ASM08AF04001 ≈ ₺282. Adding a second break so the BEC and CAN tap separate from the 4-core costs ASM08AM04001 + another ASM08AF04001 — but these field-assembly bodies take a single cable gland, so the BEC pair and CAN pair must merge into one jacket before the connector regardless. Unless that junction genuinely needs to come apart, solder plus adhesive-lined heat-shrink is more vibration-tolerant, better sealed, and free.

Keep the pin assignment identical at both ends of every run. With two connectors in one harness it is very easy to mirror one and feed 5 V into CAN_H.

Bike CAN bus

The bike broadcasts everything the puck's own sensors can't see — pack voltage, per-cell balance, motor current, state of charge, rider controls. Reading it costs one 3.3 V transceiver and two wires.

Never connect USB and the bike at the same time Once CANH/CANL are spliced in, the puck's ground is the bike's ground — shared through the BEC's negative rail. Attaching a mains-earthed computer ties the traction battery's negative to protective earth through your machine. Worse, the BEC drives 5 V into the XIAO's 5V (VUSB) pin; plug a USB host in on top of that and two 5 V rails collide on the same node.

Unplug the M8 4-pin bike connector before you attach USB. It carries +5 V, GND, CANH and CANL together, so pulling it isolates both hazards in one action — it replaces the 2-pin CAN-only break earlier revisions called for. Read telemetry over BLE instead of serial — the puck is a BLE device; use what it already does.
Remove the module's 120 Ω terminator before you connect it Nearly every SN65HVD230 breakout carries a terminating resistor across CANH/CANL — on the Alkatronik board it is the SMD part marked 121 sitting between the two CAN pads. ISO 11898-2 puts 120 Ω only at the two ends of a bus, and the bike is already terminated at both: measure across the pair with the bike off and you get 60 Ω. Adding a third resistor makes it 40 Ω, which degrades reception and throws errors on a bus you do not own.

Desolder it (or crush it — nothing else shares that net), then confirm the module reads open across CANH/CANL before wiring it in.

Listen-only, in firmware and by intent The ESP32-S3's TWAI controller runs in TWAI_MODE_LISTEN_ONLY: it never transmits and never even ACKs, so no firmware bug can disturb the bike's controller. Bring-up is non-fatal — if the transceiver is unplugged the puck still boots and streams GPS/IMU exactly as before, and the CAN block in the telemetry packet stays zeroed with its live bit clear.
The bus, once you find it 250 kbit/s, standard 11-bit IDs, 15 messages, ~92 frames/s. Signal decodes and the evidence behind each one live in tools/talaria.dbc; tools/can_sniff.py is the host-side kit that produced them. Three fields look like something they are not — 0x103[0] is state of health (pinned at 100), 0x202[6:8] is a free-running counter and not an odometer, and 0x490[0] tracks ride mode rather than temperature. No odometer was found on the bus.

0x490[0] turned out to be two fields, which is why it correlated with mode so strongly without ever being only mode: bits 5:3 echo the ride mode and bits 2:0 carry the regen level, 1–4. Verified 2026-07-30 over 10 button presses in two captures, one of them a descending 4→3→2→1 sweep so it cannot be a counter that happened to line up. Mask 3 bits, not the low nibble — in Eco bit 3 is set, so regen 1 reads 0x09.

Build sequence

Bench-verify every module before final soldering — debugging inside a stacked sandwich is misery.

Light build: steps 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 — skip step 4 (GPS) entirely. Full build: all nine in order. Because the Full build is additive, you can ship a Light build now and come back to step 4 later without unsoldering anything.

  1. Flash-test the XIAO

    Arduino IDE → add ESP32 boards (Boards Manager URL: espressif/arduino-esp32), select XIAO_ESP32S3. Upload Blink. Confirm the serial monitor works at 115200.

  2. Breadboard the I²C sensors — this is the go/no-go gate

    Wire the 10DOF board, run an I²C scanner at 100 kHz. Expect 0x28 (BNO055) and 0x76 (BMP280). Then hammer the bus: read the BNO055 continuously for 10 minutes straight and confirm zero dropped/hung transactions.

    Do not proceed until this is clean. The clock-stretch issue above has no UART fallback on this board — if the bus won't hold steady on a breadboard, it will not hold on a vibrating handlebar, and you need to know that now, not after the case is glued.

    ✅ Passed 2026-07-21 — 59,722 reads / 0 errors / 10 min @ 100 kHz, IMUPLUS active, max transaction 2.58 ms. Sketch: firmware/i2c_gate/.

  3. Prove the fusion output

    Adafruit BNO055 library → set OPERATION_MODE_IMUPLUS (6-axis) and read getQuat(). You should see live quaternions with no calibration dance. This is the moment the project becomes real: those quaternions feed the same reference-frame math as the Tripper app — and unlike the MPU-6050's DMP, you didn't have to fight for them.

    ✅ Passed 2026-07-21 — library path up in IMUPLUS, live quaternions + linear accel on the breadboard (test sketch since folded into firmware/tripper_puck/). Bonus: MON-VER unmasked the "NEO-M8Z" GPS as genuine u-blox 8 silicon (ROM 3.01, SPG 3.01, PROTVER 18, GPS/GLO/BDS/QZSS).

  4. Get a GPS fix — Full build only, skip for Light

    Wire GPS to UART, run a TinyGPS++ example near a window or outside — first cold fix can take 1–5 min. Then send the UBX commands to raise the rate to 5–10 Hz and baud to 115200 (one-time config, saved to the module's flash/battery-backed RAM).

    ✅ Fix acquired 2026-07-21 — NMEA + TinyGPS++ verified, MON-VER confirms genuine u-blox 8 (ROM 3.01, PROTVER 18, GPS/GLO/BDS/QZSS). Config applied & verified same day (firmware/gps_config_5hz/): CFG-RATE 200 ms ACKed, read-back confirmed, saved to BBR, measured 5.0 GGA/s at 115200. Production firmware re-applies at every boot in case the backup battery ever drains. Bench extra that got promoted: 0.91" SSD1306 OLED at 0x3C — bench-proven on the shared bus, since moved to its own Wire1 (D3/D10) for the handlebar run — now integral to firmware/tripper_puck/ (clock/data screens, hold toggle, zero progress UI).

  5. Bring up the CAN bus

    Remove the SN65HVD230's onboard 121 terminator (see the Bike CAN section), wire CTX→D8 / CRX→D9, and tap CANH/CANL at the controller. Flash firmware/can_probe/ — it brings TWAI up at 250 kbit/s listen-only and reports a decoded dashboard on the OLED and over BLE (device Tripper-CAN, Nordic UART).

    Read wirelessly, not over serial — once CANH/CANL are spliced in you must never also attach a USB host (see the warning in the Bike CAN section). Zero frames on the OLED almost always means CANH/CANL are swapped: harmless, just swap them.

    ✅ Verified on the bench 2026-07-23 — Talaria bus decoded end to end: 250 kbit/s, 15 IDs, and the module reproduced the reference numbers (81 %, 64.3 V, kickstand, ride mode) captured independently with a USB-CAN dongle.

  6. Integrate the firmware

    Flash the sketch for your build — firmware/tripper_light/ or firmware/tripper_puck/ (architecture below). Bench-test by shaking the puck and watching the telemetry stream in a BLE scanner. The Light build needs only Adafruit BNO055, Adafruit BMP280 and NimBLE-Arduino; the Full build adds Adafruit SSD1306 and TinyGPSPlus.

    ✅ e-bike edition firmware running (2026-07-21, CAN added 2026-07-23) — firmware/tripper_puck/: BLE service (telemetry 5 Hz / status 1 Hz / control), OLED screens (clock / data / bike-CAN, stepped by D1), marker button, 10 s mount-zero (reference quaternion, persisted to NVS, applied to display and telemetry), GPS autoconfig at boot, and listen-only CAN folded into the 70-byte telemetry packet. Phone-verified with LightBlue: connect, notify stream, control writes all working.

  7. Solder the stack

    • Shorten all wires to final lengths; silicone wire tolerates vibration best.
    • BEC +out → the XIAO 5V (VUSB) pin, BEC −out → GND. Heat-shrink everything; the BEC's input taps the bike's pack / accessory line.
    • Light build: put a 2-pin connector in the CANH/CANL run so you can isolate the bike before ever attaching USB to flash. One box: XIAO, SN65HVD230 on a short pigtail, Gravity board on foam.
    • Full build: terminate the bike run in the M8 4-pin connector (male on the panel), same purpose. Terminate the handlebar run in the M12 8-pin connector — fit its passives while the boards are still accessible: 2.2 kΩ OLED pull-ups and the 10 kΩ / 100 nF button networks at the rear end, the 100 µF bulk cap at the bar end. Handlebar box: OLED window, both buttons in the lid, GPS under a thin lid section.

  8. Enclosure & mounting

    • Light build — one enclosure at the rear near the controller tap: ESP32, CAN module, sensor board. Nothing goes on the handlebar, so there is no second case, no bar cabling and no antenna placement problem.
    • Full build — two enclosures, split rear / handlebar: the ESP32, CAN module and sensor board ride at the rear; the OLED, two buttons and GPS sit at the handlebar (the GPS wants sky view, the screen wants your eyeline).
    • Rear case ≈ 55 × 40 × 22 mm; cutouts: USB-C (flashing), the CAN + BEC leads, and on Full the M8 4-pin panel receptacle for the bike plus the M12 8-pin for the handlebar run. Check your wall thickness against the connector's thread depth — M8/M12 panel jacks typically want ≤ 2–3 mm.
    • Handlebar case (Full only): OLED window, two button cutouts, a sky-facing 1.5 mm lid section over the GPS patch, and the M12 cable entry. No USB port here.
    • Sensor board sits on the 2 mm foam pad — isolates handlebar buzz from the accelerometer.
    • GPS antenna under a 1.5 mm lid section (plastic is RF-transparent), ESP32 at the opposite end.
    • Mount the cases rigidly to the bar/frame (the foam is inside, not outside) — same rule as the phone.

  9. Field-verify against Tripper

    Ride with the puck and the phone app recording simultaneously. The CAN block should carry live battery %, pack voltage, ride mode and regen level, and orientation should track the bike's lean. Compare in the app — the BLE stream is the only data path, there is no file to import.

    Full build: distances should agree within a few percent and the puck's 5 Hz track should look smoother in corners than the phone's 1 Hz. Light build: position comes from the phone, so there is no second track to compare — verify instead that orientation, g-force, barometric altitude and the whole CAN block land in the recording, and that caps reads 0x08 so the app knows to keep using phone GPS.

Firmware architecture

Arduino (C++). One loop, three rates, no RTOS needed. Two sketches: tripper_light/ and tripper_puck/.

Two sketches, one wire format tripper_puck/ is the Full build. tripper_light/ is the same firmware with the GPS, OLED and button code removed outright — not disabled by a flag, so the Light build carries no dead weight and needs two fewer libraries. What stays identical: service UUIDs, the 70-byte telemetry packet, the 14-byte status packet, and the whole CAN decode.

A Light puck fills the GPS block with zeros and leaves both flags bits clear — indistinguishable from a Full puck that hasn't got a fix yet, which the app already handles — so no app branch is required. The status packet's last byte, previously reserved, now carries capability bits: 0x08 from Light, 0x0F from Full. Zero means firmware older than the field, which is why "has CAN" is an explicit bit rather than assumed.

The cost of two sketches is drift. Each is standalone, so the packet structs and CAN decode exist twice. Anything touching the wire format must land in both — README.md → Build & flash carries a one-liner that diffs the two field lists and should print nothing.

No SD, no CSV, no auto-record state machine — those moved to the phone. The puck is a stateless sensor: it streams, the app records and decides when a ride is running (and tells the puck over the control characteristic, which is what flips the OLED into trip-time mode).

Buttons — Full build only

The Light build has none: a marker arrives as control write 0x01 and mount-zero as 0x02, both from the app. On Full, 0x01 is only an ack for a press the button already counted; on Light it originates the marker, since nothing else can.

Both sit on pin-change ISRs with 50 ms debounce, so presses land even while the loop is stalled. Each discriminates click from hold the same way: the ISR timestamps the press and counts a click only if the release lands inside 600 ms, so a long hold never also fires a click.

D1 (12 mm glove button) — click steps to the next OLED screen · 3 s hold toggles auto-cycling. Screens do not rotate on their own by default: on a moving bike the screen you picked should stay put.

D2 (HW-483) — click drops a marker into the telemetry packet · 10 s hold zeroes roll/pitch/yaw and saves the reference quaternion to NVS.

Status — all on the OLED (Full build)

The Light build has no indicator at all — not even a status LED — so everything below is Full-only. That is deliberate rather than a gap: the phone is already in the rider's pocket running the app, and the OLED exists to save you pulling it out. A Light puck reports its health entirely through the 1 Hz BLE status packet.

There is no status LED (the NeoPixel was dropped). Everything reads off the 0.91" screen: a fix dot (hollow → solid on lock), LINK/ADV for the BLE state, satellite bars, MARK and ZEROED splashes, and the bike-CAN screen's own no data / off indicator. During a ride (app-driven) the whole display inverts and a trip-time screen joins the rotation.

On-air format — the BLE telemetry packet

There is no file. The data leaves as a 70-byte packed little-endian sample notified at 5 Hz — GPS, quaternion, linear/​max g, marker, then the bike-CAN block (speed, rpm, power, current, pack voltage, battery %, throttle demand, cell high/low, kickstand, ride mode, regen level). The app's ExternalSensorSource feeds it into the same recorder pipeline the phone sensors use. Full offset table: see README.md → BLE protocol.

Identical on both builds, byte for byte. On a Light puck the GPS fields are zero and flags bits 0–1 are clear, gpsTimeMs is 0xFFFFFFFF and hdop_c is 9999 — the app must gate on those bits anyway, because a Full puck looks the same indoors. Read caps in the status packet to know whether position will ever arrive, and fall back to phone GPS when bit 0x01 is clear.

Power

No battery. The puck runs off the bike through a 5 V BEC, so the only question is whether the BEC has margin — and it has a lot.

ConsumerTypical draw

The BEC is oversized on purpose — headroom for inrush at boot and for a phone charging off the same outlet if you ever add one. The one thing to check on your bike: that the BEC's input line stays live at standstill and doesn't cut at low load, or the puck drops out every time you stop.

The Bluetooth link to Tripper

The puck is a live sensor for the app — this is the product, not a future phase.

One BLE service (Tripper-DL1), three characteristics:

CharacteristicDirectionPayload
telemetrynotify → phone70-byte packed sample @ 5 Hz: fix, lat, lon, alt, speed, course, quat, g, baro, marker + the bike-CAN block
statusnotify → phone14 bytes @ 1 Hz: fix quality, sats, uptime, temperature, marker
controlwrite ← phonemarker ack · zero attitude · identify · ride state (active + elapsed)

App side: an ExternalSensorSource that feeds the same recorder pipeline the phone sensors use today — the dashboard, Dynamic Island, overlays, and exports all work unchanged, now with 5 Hz GPS and the bike's own battery/drivetrain data. The phone's Marker button stamps markers into both logs. Exact byte layout and control opcodes: README.md → BLE protocol.

Pitfalls — read before soldering

  • BEC vs USB — never both: the BEC feeds 5 V into VUSB. Plugging USB in while the BEC is powered collides two 5 V rails and, with the CAN tap connected, bridges the bike's ground to mains earth. Unplug the M8 4-pin bike connector before flashing — it kills power and CAN in one pull.
  • Don't share one I²C bus with the handlebar: the OLED belongs on Wire1 (D3/D10), not on the sensor bus. Hanging a metre of cable off D4/D5 risks the BNO055, and a flaky sensor bus costs data — a flaky display costs nothing.
  • Phantom button presses: a metre of wire on a 45 kΩ internal pull-up picks up controller noise. Fit the 10 kΩ / 100 nF network on D1 and D2 at the ESP32 end before you blame the firmware.
  • Torn or blank OLED after the extension: drop OLED_BURST_HZ to 100 kHz first, then check the 2.2 kΩ pull-ups, then check that SDA and SCL aren't twisted with each other.
  • CAN terminator: remove the module's onboard 121 (120 Ω) resistor — the bike's bus is already terminated at 60 Ω and a third makes it 40 Ω. Measure open across CANH/CANL before wiring in.
  • CANH/CANL swap: harmless but silent — you just get zero frames. If the probe shows no data, swap the pair first.
  • BEC dropout at standstill: confirm the BEC's input line stays live when the bike is stopped, or the puck reboots every time you stop.
  • GPS near ESP32: keep antenna ≥ 15 mm from the module and USB cable; test fix time before gluing anything.
  • Fake u-blox: ultra-cheap marketplace "M8N" boards are often older chips reflashed — buy from drone shops; verify with u-center over serial.
  • Vibration: foam under the sensor board or your g-force column is engine buzz.
  • Waterproofing: conformal coat the boards or accept "shower-proof"; keep the USB port plugged with a rubber stopper.
